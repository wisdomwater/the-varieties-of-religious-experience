# Day 52 — The Experience of Being Received

*There are moments when the deepest assurance comes not from what we have accomplished, but from the feeling that we are accepted before we have accomplished anything at all.*

Among the experiences accompanying religious conversion, few are more remarkable than the sudden emergence of assurance. A person who has long struggled with feelings of inadequacy or spiritual estrangement may discover an unexpected sense of acceptance, as though the distance separating the individual from the divine has been overcome. The experience is frequently described not as a successful achievement, but as the reception of something freely given.

The French Protestant Adolphe Monod provides an illuminating example. During a period of profound melancholy in Naples in 1827, he became convinced that neither his reason nor his will could deliver him from his suffering. The very faculties upon which he had depended seemed incapable of correcting the condition that afflicted them. He eventually turned toward the Christian promise of divine assistance, surrendering his claims to personal merit and trusting himself to a power beyond his own resources.

Monod did not report that his melancholy immediately disappeared. Rather, something altered in his relationship to it. Hope entered his consciousness, and the suffering that had previously governed his life lost much of its power to overwhelm him. His experience illustrates how religious assurance may transform the meaning of distress without necessarily removing its outward causes.

Another account, collected by Edwin Starbuck, describes an individual who attended a religious meeting and struggled unsuccessfully for two hours to obtain spiritual peace. The minister suggested that the difficulty arose from an unwillingness to surrender completely. Yet no assurance came during the meeting. Only later, alone at home, did the individual finally relinquish further effort and entrust the matter to God. An immediate feeling of peace followed, which the person understood as the certainty of divine acceptance.

What these experiences share is the transition from anxious self-evaluation toward a sense of being sustained by something greater than the individual will. The person no longer experiences spiritual security as a reward that must continually be earned or defended. Instead, assurance becomes the ground from which a different relationship with existence may develop.

We must distinguish the experience itself from the explanation offered for its origin. Psychology can describe the disappearance of anxiety and the emergence of confidence, while theology may interpret these changes as the reception of divine grace. Whether these accounts ultimately converge remains an open question.

Yet the psychological transformation is significant in its own right. The individual who previously experienced life as a continual struggle for acceptance may come to inhabit it with a new willingness, discovering that the possibility of peace does not always depend upon the successful resolution of every imperfection.
