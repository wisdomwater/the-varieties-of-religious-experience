# Day 60 — The Enlargement of Compassion

*When the boundaries of our concern expand, the suffering of others ceases to be something distant from our own experience of life.*

Human sympathy ordinarily moves within familiar boundaries. We feel tenderness toward those we love, concern for those who resemble us, and compassion for suffering that awakens our imagination. Yet these feelings are rarely distributed equally. Differences of temperament, social position, belief, and personal history often determine whom we regard as worthy of our affection and whom we find easy to disregard.

Among the most striking characteristics of saintliness is the enlargement of these boundaries. Religious experience sometimes produces an expansive tenderness that reaches beyond family, friendship, and ordinary social attachments. Individuals describe discovering a new affection for humanity, accompanied by a diminished tendency toward resentment, judgment, and hostility.

The psychologist Edwin Starbuck collected numerous accounts in which conversion was followed by a noticeable increase in sympathy. Some individuals reported becoming more affectionate toward their families, while others described reconciliation with people toward whom they had previously felt anger. These changes were not always the result of deliberate moral instruction. They often appeared as spontaneous expressions of a newly awakened emotional disposition.

Sarah Edwards, the wife of the theologian Jonathan Edwards, described an extraordinary experience of universal goodwill. She felt such powerful affection for humanity that even the thought of encountering enemies who wished to harm her seemed incapable of awakening hatred. Her experience was accompanied by a heightened appreciation of the importance of human relationships and the obligations of kindness toward others.

We might suppose that such tenderness follows logically from religious beliefs concerning the divine parenthood of humanity. If all persons belong to one spiritual family, then compassion might naturally extend beyond the divisions separating them. Yet similar expressions of universal goodwill appear in Buddhism, Hinduism, and Stoicism, suggesting that the experience cannot be explained exclusively through any one theological doctrine.

There may be a more general psychological relationship between expansive states of consciousness and sympathetic feeling. When individuals experience profound joy, gratitude, or spiritual exaltation, the usual preoccupation with their private interests may temporarily diminish. The world becomes less threatening, and other people may appear less as competitors or obstacles than as fellow participants in a shared existence.

This enlargement of compassion raises questions that extend beyond individual happiness. If our ordinary hostilities arise partly from habits of emotional separation, then a transformation of those habits could alter the way we organize our relationships and communities.

We should not assume that every generous feeling produces wise action, or that compassion removes the need for discernment. Nevertheless, the capacity to experience another person's welfare as something genuinely important reveals a remarkable possibility within human nature.

The saintly temperament invites us to consider whether the limits of our sympathy are as fixed as they ordinarily appear, and what human relationships might become if our capacity for tenderness were allowed a wider expression.
