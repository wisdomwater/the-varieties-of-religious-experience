# Day 66 — When Devotion Becomes Fanaticism

*The strength of our convictions does not guarantee their wisdom, and devotion to what we believe is good may sometimes lead us to do great harm.*

Having examined the admirable qualities of saintliness, we must now consider its possible failures. Religious devotion can inspire extraordinary kindness, courage, and self-sacrifice, yet the same intensity of conviction may also become a source of intolerance and cruelty. If we wish to understand religious experience fairly, we cannot praise its finest expressions while overlooking the damage that has sometimes followed from its excesses.

The difficulty becomes particularly apparent when individuals believe themselves responsible for defending the honor of God. An insult directed toward their religious convictions may cease to seem merely a disagreement between human beings. It becomes an offense against the highest reality they recognize, demanding correction, punishment, or even destruction of those regarded as enemies of the divine.

History supplies troubling illustrations. Catherine of Siena, despite her passionate desire to end warfare among Christians, supported the idea of a crusade against the Turks. Martin Luther did not publicly protest the brutal treatment of Anabaptist leaders, while Oliver Cromwell interpreted military victories over his enemies as occasions for thanksgiving to God. In such cases, religious conviction became entangled with political interests, inherited hostilities, and the justification of violence.

We must be careful, however, not to attribute every atrocity committed in religion's name to religious experience itself. Human beings possess powerful instincts toward tribal loyalty, aggression, and domination, quite apart from their spiritual convictions. Religious institutions may provide these impulses with a sacred vocabulary, allowing ordinary struggles for power to appear as obligations imposed by heaven.

There is nevertheless a danger within devotion that cannot be dismissed so easily. When an individual's religious enthusiasm becomes sufficiently intense, it may overwhelm the moderating influence of sympathy, reflection, and doubt. If the conception of God directing that enthusiasm is narrow, authoritarian, or preoccupied with receiving honor, the devotee may regard cruelty as an expression of loyalty.

This reveals an important limitation of sincerity as a moral standard. A person may be utterly convinced of the righteousness of an action and yet remain profoundly mistaken about its consequences. The willingness to sacrifice everything for an ideal does not establish that the ideal deserves such allegiance.

We must therefore consider the intellectual understanding that accompanies spiritual passion. Powerful devotion joined to a generous conception of humanity may produce remarkable goodness. The same devotion, confined within a rigid and hostile worldview, may become destructive.

The essential question is not simply whether we believe deeply, but what our beliefs lead us to become. When devotion requires us to abandon compassion or regard others as enemies unworthy of consideration, its intensity should give us reason for greater scrutiny rather than greater confidence.

Religious experience reaches its highest possibilities only when the energy of conviction remains open to the guidance of understanding, humility, and humane judgment.
