# Day 69 — The Saint in an Imperfect World

*The highest ideals may appear impractical in the world as it exists, yet without them, the world may never become something better.*

We have reached a difficult question in our examination of saintliness. Even if we admire the saint's generosity, humility, courage, and tenderness, can we reasonably regard such a person as an ideal for humanity? The world in which we live is not composed entirely of people who respond to kindness with kindness. It contains aggression, dishonesty, competition, and the persistent struggle for advantage. In such an environment, the saint's gentle disposition may sometimes appear poorly suited to the demands of existence.

The philosopher Friedrich Nietzsche offers one of the strongest challenges to the saintly ideal. He celebrates vitality, independence, strength, and the ability to impose one's will upon circumstances. From this perspective, the saint's meekness and willingness to suffer may appear as symptoms of weakness rather than expressions of moral excellence. A society composed entirely of such individuals might seem incapable of defending itself against those willing to exercise power.

Yet we should examine more carefully what we mean when we call one form of character superior to another. An ideal cannot be judged independently of the circumstances in which it operates. A quality that proves useful in one environment may become harmful in another, while conduct regarded as impractical today might be perfectly suited to a different social order.

Consider a community in which every individual acts with genuine fairness, sympathy, and goodwill. Within such a society, aggression would serve little purpose. There would be no advantage in intimidation, no necessity for constant suspicion, and no reason to protect oneself through the domination of others. The saintly character would be remarkably well adapted to such an environment.

The aggressive individual, by contrast, would introduce the very hostility that this community had overcome. His strength might enable him to prevail in conflict, but his conduct would diminish the quality of the society itself. The saint therefore represents, at least in principle, a type of character suited to the highest form of human association we can imagine.

The difficulty lies in the distance between this ideal society and our actual circumstances. We cannot assume that gentleness will always overcome violence or that trust will invariably be rewarded. The saint who refuses to acknowledge these realities may suffer unnecessarily or fail to protect others who depend upon him.

Nevertheless, our judgment of success must extend beyond immediate survival and personal advantage. The apostle Paul, for example, was executed, yet his influence upon subsequent generations demonstrates how profoundly a life may succeed in historical terms despite its apparent worldly defeat.

The same possibility applies more generally to the saintly character. An individual may fail to secure personal comfort or safety while nevertheless helping to establish new standards of compassion, justice, and human dignity. The influence of such a life may continue long after the circumstances that seemed to defeat it have disappeared.

We must therefore resist the temptation to measure every human ideal solely by its usefulness within the present arrangement of society. Some lives are valuable precisely because they reveal the limitations of that arrangement and point toward possibilities not yet realized.

The saint may not always be the most successful participant in the world as it stands. Yet the qualities embodied in saintliness may help determine what kind of world future generations will inherit. To judge such a life adequately, we must consider not only how well it adapts to existing conditions, but also how deeply it contributes to their transformation.
