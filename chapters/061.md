# Day 61 — The Generosity of an Open Heart

*The measure of generosity may be found not only in what we are willing to give, but in how freely we allow another person's welfare to matter to us.*

Compassion becomes especially significant when it moves beyond feeling and begins to shape our actions. We may sincerely wish others well while remaining reluctant to surrender our comfort, possessions, or sense of personal importance. Yet among those whose religious convictions have profoundly transformed their character, we sometimes encounter a generosity that appears almost indifferent to these ordinary restraints. The needs of others acquire an immediacy that makes giving seem less like a sacrifice than a natural response.

An unusual illustration appears in the life of the Polish mystic Andrzej Towiański. A friend once encountered him in the rain, allowing an enthusiastic dog to jump upon him and cover his clothing with mud. When questioned about his willingness to tolerate the animal's behavior, Towiański explained that rejecting its affectionate greeting would cause a greater injury than the damage to his coat. He understood even this seemingly insignificant encounter as an opportunity to recognize and honor another living creature.

The incident may strike us as excessive, yet it reveals an important feature of expansive generosity. Towiański's attention was directed less toward the inconvenience he suffered than toward the possibility of causing unnecessary distress. His concern extended beyond the usual boundaries of human relationships, suggesting that spiritual tenderness could embrace forms of life ordinarily excluded from our moral consideration.

A more demanding example appears in the autobiography of Richard Weaver, an English coal miner who became an evangelist. Weaver had once been inclined toward drinking and fighting, and his conversion did not immediately eliminate these habits. Nevertheless, he later described an encounter in which a fellow worker named Tom attempted to seize a wagon from a younger worker.

Weaver intervened to protect the boy, but Tom responded with insults and violence. Rather than retaliating, Weaver allowed himself to be struck repeatedly, declaring his forgiveness even as the attack continued. When they met again the following Monday, Tom approached him in tears and asked to be forgiven. Weaver extended his hand, and the two returned to their work.

We need not conclude that accepting physical violence is always wise or morally required. Indeed, Weaver's initial intervention shows that generosity need not exclude resistance to wrongdoing. What makes the episode significant is his refusal to allow another person's hostility to determine the limits of his goodwill.

These examples suggest that generosity involves more than the distribution of material resources. It may also consist in granting attention, patience, dignity, and forgiveness where ordinary self-interest would encourage withdrawal or retaliation.

The saintly character sometimes reveals a freedom from the continual calculation of personal advantage. Such freedom is not without its dangers, for indiscriminate giving may become imprudent or invite exploitation. Yet it also raises an important possibility: that goodness may spread through human relationships when someone becomes willing to respond to another's needs with a generosity greater than circumstances seem to demand.
