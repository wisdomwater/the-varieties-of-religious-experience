# Day 62 — Simplicity and the Purification of Desire

*As our deepest values become clearer, we may discover that much of what once occupied our attention no longer deserves the place we have given it.*

One of the distinctive tendencies of the saintly character is a growing desire for simplicity. As religious conviction becomes the organizing center of consciousness, the individual may develop an unusual sensitivity to anything that seems inconsistent with that conviction. Habits, possessions, pleasures, and social customs once accepted without question begin to appear unnecessary or even troublesome. The desire for a more unified life gradually alters the way ordinary choices are understood.

This transformation is closely related to what religious traditions have called purity. We should understand the term here not merely as the avoidance of particular moral offenses, but as the effort to bring the various dimensions of life into harmony with a governing ideal. The individual becomes increasingly unwilling to maintain a division between deeply held convictions and the habits of everyday existence.

The English evangelist Billy Bray offers a useful illustration. Having previously been a heavy drinker and smoker, Bray came to regard his attachment to tobacco as incompatible with his religious commitments. He described an inward conviction that smoking had become an object of excessive devotion, and an encounter with a woman named Mary Hawke reinforced his growing uneasiness. Eventually, he threw his tobacco into the fire and crushed his pipe beneath his foot.

Yet the decision did not immediately eliminate the difficulty. Bray acknowledged that he continued to experience strong desires to resume smoking, even while remaining committed to his renunciation. His account illustrates how the purification of desire may involve a gradual reorganization of habits rather than their instantaneous disappearance.

A related impulse appeared among the early Quakers, whose founder George Fox rejected customary gestures of social deference. Removing one's hat before a superior or using titles that distinguished people according to rank seemed to him inconsistent with the spiritual equality he professed. What others regarded as harmless conventions became, in his understanding, expressions of a dishonesty he could no longer comfortably practice.

Such examples reveal that simplicity is not necessarily concerned with possessing fewer things. It may arise from a desire to eliminate contradictions between belief and conduct, bringing the whole of life under the guidance of a more consistent understanding of what is valuable.

There are dangers in this tendency. A heightened sensitivity to inconsistency may develop into excessive scrupulousness, and the pursuit of purity may become so restrictive that ordinary relationships and responsibilities suffer. Not every discomfort indicates a moral problem, and the removal of complexity is not always an improvement.

Nevertheless, the desire for a simpler and more unified existence remains an important feature of spiritual transformation. When certain purposes become deeply meaningful, other desires may gradually lose their appeal. The individual begins to discover that freedom can sometimes emerge through relinquishment, as attention and energy are released from commitments that no longer serve the life one most deeply wishes to inhabit.
