# Day 27 — The Loss of Life's Sweetness

*The joy we find in living is not produced by circumstances alone, but depends upon an inward capacity to experience their goodness.*

There is a form of suffering more difficult to comprehend than ordinary sadness. In sadness, we remain conscious of something valuable that has been lost, and our grief bears witness to the affection we once possessed. But there are conditions in which the very capacity for pleasure seems to disappear. The familiar objects of happiness remain before us, yet they no longer awaken the feelings that once gave them meaning.

The psychologist Théodule Ribot has proposed the term *anhedonia* to describe this remarkable condition. He recounts the case of a young girl who, following an illness, could no longer find pleasure in playing with her doll or affectionately responding to her parents. Another individual continued attending the theater out of habit, yet experienced no enjoyment in the performances. Even thoughts of his home and family stirred no more emotion than an abstract mathematical proposition.

Such accounts reveal how much of our ordinary experience depends upon the mysterious cooperation of feeling. We imagine that beautiful surroundings, loving relationships, and agreeable circumstances naturally produce happiness. Yet these things possess no power to guarantee the emotional response we expect from them. When our inward capacity for enjoyment is diminished, the world itself seems strangely altered, although its outward appearance remains unchanged.

The Catholic philosopher Father Gratry described a period of nervous exhaustion during which this loss of feeling extended into his religious imagination. His former understanding of heaven no longer offered consolation. The very words associated with happiness, affection, and divine goodness seemed emptied of their familiar significance. He could remember the ideas, but could no longer enter into the experiences they represented.

We must recognize that such conditions may arise through illness and disturbances of the nervous system, and that their presence does not establish any final truth about the worth of existence. Nevertheless, they reveal something important about human consciousness. Our ability to experience meaning is not entirely under the command of our reasoning or will.

The ordinary sweetness of living, which we so easily take for granted, is itself a remarkable gift. Its temporary or prolonged absence shows how deeply our understanding of reality depends upon the feelings through which reality becomes meaningful to us.

When that sweetness disappears, the human being confronts not simply an unhappy world, but a world in which the familiar sources of happiness have fallen strangely silent.
