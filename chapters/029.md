# Day 29 — The Fear Beneath Existence

*There are moments when fear reveals not a particular danger, but the unsettling discovery of how little security our existence possesses.*

Ordinarily, fear arises in response to something we can identify. We fear illness, poverty, rejection, or the loss of someone dear to us. Such anxieties may be painful, but they remain connected to recognizable circumstances. There is, however, another kind of fear that seems to reach beneath these particular concerns. It is the sudden realization that our entire existence rests upon conditions over which we possess remarkably little control.

A correspondent once described to me an experience of this kind. While entering a dressing room in the twilight, he was suddenly overcome by an overwhelming terror of his own existence. The memory of a severely disabled young patient he had previously encountered in an asylum appeared vividly before his mind. He recognized, with a force that shook his entire being, that nothing guaranteed his own immunity from such a condition.

The experience transformed his understanding of the world. For months afterward, he lived with a profound sense of insecurity, fearing solitude and finding ordinary life strangely difficult to comprehend. He wondered how other people could move through their days so peacefully, apparently unaware of the vulnerability that had become painfully obvious to him. Even his cheerful mother seemed to inhabit a reality different from his own.

What makes this account especially significant is that the fear did not depend upon an immediate external threat. The conditions of his life had not suddenly changed. Rather, his consciousness had become intensely aware of possibilities that had always existed beneath the surface of ordinary experience.

We should not mistake the overwhelming intensity of such fear for proof that the universe is fundamentally hostile. The sufferer's nervous condition undoubtedly contributed to the experience. Yet the vulnerability that frightened him was not entirely imaginary. Human beings are fragile, and even our most familiar sense of security cannot guarantee protection from every misfortune.

The correspondent found some measure of support in passages of Scripture that spoke of divine refuge and consolation. Their importance lay in offering him something to hold against a fear that ordinary reasoning could not easily quiet.

Here we begin to understand why religion may become profoundly significant to those who experience the insecurity of existence so directly. They seek something more enduring than reassurance about particular circumstances. They long for a foundation of trust that can remain meaningful even when the familiar securities of life have fallen away.
