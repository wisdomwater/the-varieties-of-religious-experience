# Day 48 — When Change Comes Suddenly

*Sometimes a single moment accomplishes what years of effort have been unable to achieve, leaving us astonished by the transformation of our own hearts.*

We have considered the gradual development of religious character and the possibility that important changes may be prepared outside our immediate awareness. Yet the history of religious experience also contains accounts in which transformation appears to occur with extraordinary suddenness. An individual who has struggled for years with uncertainty, guilt, or conflicting desires may experience an abrupt alteration of consciousness, after which the entire direction of life seems fundamentally changed.

Let us return to Henry Alline, whose prolonged inward suffering we examined earlier. For many months, Alline had attempted to reconcile his religious aspirations with the habits and social attachments of his former life. He prayed, examined his conscience, and struggled to establish a sense of spiritual security, yet remained tormented by the conviction that something essential was missing.

On March 26, 1775, this struggle reached its unexpected conclusion. While returning home after wandering in the fields near sunset, Alline experienced what seemed to him an inward voice questioning the effectiveness of his repeated efforts to obtain salvation. The experience brought about an overwhelming change in his consciousness. The fear and spiritual misery that had governed his existence gave way to an extraordinary sense of divine acceptance and joy.

What followed was more than a temporary improvement in his mood. Alline understood himself to have received a calling to preach the gospel, and he soon began a life of devoted religious service. His former amusements lost their attraction, while the spiritual concerns that had previously tormented him became the organizing purpose of his existence.

The psychological significance of this transformation lies partly in its apparent discontinuity. Immediately before the experience, Alline remained imprisoned within the same distress that had troubled him for months. Immediately afterward, he possessed a sense of assurance that his previous efforts had failed to produce. To the individual undergoing such a change, it may seem that a power beyond his ordinary will has intervened.

We must be careful, however, not to confuse the suddenness of an experience with proof of its supernatural origin. The psychologist may investigate whether previously developing mental processes help explain the transformation, while the religious believer may interpret the same event as an operation of divine grace. The experience itself does not automatically settle the disagreement between these explanations.

Nor should we conclude that an instantaneous conversion is necessarily more valuable than gradual growth. The manner in which a new life begins must be distinguished from the character of the life that follows.

What remains remarkable is the human capacity for such abrupt reorganization. Convictions that once struggled unsuccessfully for influence may suddenly acquire commanding authority, and a person who seemed unable to change may discover an entirely different way of living. Whatever its ultimate explanation, this possibility deserves a central place in our understanding of religious experience.
