# Day 20 — The Courage to Trust

*Trust is not the certainty that life will unfold as we desire, but the willingness to live without being governed by fear.*

Much of human life is spent anticipating what might go wrong. We worry about illness, financial misfortune, the loss of those we love, and the countless uncertainties that accompany our existence. Such concerns arise naturally from our desire for security, yet they can become so deeply rooted in consciousness that we begin to experience life primarily as a collection of dangers to be avoided. Our efforts to protect ourselves may gradually diminish our capacity to live freely.

The advocates of healthy-mindedness offer a striking alternative. They suggest that the human mind can cultivate an attitude of confidence toward existence, trusting that a greater power or deeper order sustains life beyond the limits of our personal control. For many who embrace this outlook, trust becomes more than an intellectual conviction. It develops into a living disposition, transforming the way they encounter uncertainty.

Consider the practice of entering into silence, which has become important among followers of New Thought. By withdrawing attention from surrounding distractions and directing consciousness toward an ideal of divine goodness, the individual seeks to recover a sense of inward steadiness. One account describes a man who, even amid the noise and activity of a busy office, could turn inward during moments of perplexity and wait in quiet confidence for guidance.

What interests me is not whether every impression received in such moments comes from a supernatural source, but the psychological significance of the attitude itself. The person who trusts may discover an unexpected freedom from the exhausting demands of constant self-protection. Their circumstances may remain uncertain, yet their relationship to uncertainty has changed.

We must not confuse such confidence with a guarantee of favorable outcomes. The universe does not necessarily arrange itself according to our wishes, and the conviction that we are protected from every misfortune may prove tragically mistaken.

Nevertheless, there is genuine significance in the movement from anxious anticipation toward confident participation in life. Trust may not remove uncertainty, but for those capable of experiencing it deeply, it can transform the very conditions under which uncertainty is endured.
