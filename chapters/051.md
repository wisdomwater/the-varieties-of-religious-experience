# Day 51 — Letting Go of the Struggle

*Sometimes our deepest change begins when the exhausting effort to secure our own happiness gives way to a different way of experiencing life.*

There are moments in human experience when the struggle against an unwanted condition becomes almost inseparable from the condition itself. Fear produces resistance, resistance heightens our awareness of fear, and the mind becomes increasingly occupied with the very suffering it wishes to escape. In certain forms of religious distress, this inward conflict may continue until the individual reaches a point of exhaustion, where the familiar efforts of the will can no longer be sustained.

We find a remarkable illustration in the experience of David Brainerd, the eighteenth-century missionary whose religious struggles were marked by intense self-examination. Brainerd had devoted himself to prayer, fasting, and the pursuit of salvation, yet eventually became convinced that his efforts were motivated primarily by concern for his own happiness. Even his religious devotion appeared compromised by self-interest, leaving him unable to discover a satisfactory foundation for spiritual assurance.

During a period of profound discouragement, Brainerd found that his former religious anxieties had largely disappeared. He interpreted this absence of feeling as evidence that God had abandoned him. Nevertheless, the emotional struggle that had previously occupied his consciousness seemed to have lost its force.

While walking through a grove on July 12, 1739, he experienced a sudden apprehension of divine glory. He described no external light or visible apparition, but an inward awareness of God's excellence that filled him with extraordinary joy. His attention was no longer concentrated upon his own salvation. Indeed, he became so absorbed in the contemplation of the divine that concern for himself almost disappeared entirely.

This experience presents an important psychological question. Did the exhaustion of Brainerd's former anxieties permit a new religious feeling to emerge, or did the arrival of that feeling itself overcome his distress? We cannot confidently separate these possibilities. Both processes may have contributed to the same transformation.

The essential observation is that the disappearance of one emotional condition may create an opportunity for another to assume control. When the mind ceases to be entirely occupied with its own deficiencies, interests previously obscured may acquire a new intensity.

Brainerd's experience should not be reduced to a universal prescription for abandoning effort, nor should his suffering be regarded as a necessary preparation for spiritual growth. Other conversions occur without such prolonged distress.

Yet his account reveals a curious possibility within human consciousness. The movement toward a larger life may sometimes begin when the desperate attempt to possess that life has finally exhausted itself, allowing a different source of meaning to become the center of experience.
