# Day 36 — The Struggle Between Desire and Ideal

*Knowing what we most deeply wish to become does not always give us the power to become it.*

Among the most perplexing features of human nature is the distance that sometimes separates our convictions from our conduct. We may recognize a particular course of life as worthy of our highest aspirations and sincerely desire to follow it, yet discover that our actions continue in another direction. The difficulty is not necessarily that we lack understanding. Often, we understand our situation with painful clarity, while the impulses governing our behavior remain stubbornly resistant to change.

Saint Augustine provides one of the most remarkable descriptions of this divided condition. In his *Confessions*, he recounts the struggle between his longing for spiritual transformation and his attachment to familiar pleasures. His judgment had become convinced that a different life was better, yet the habits he had cultivated over many years continued to exercise their influence. He experienced these opposing tendencies as two wills, each belonging to himself, yet neither sufficiently powerful to resolve the conflict.

What makes Augustine's testimony especially illuminating is his recognition that the desire for transformation can coexist with a fear of actually undergoing it. He wished to be freed from his attachments, but not quite yet. The prospect of surrendering what he knew remained frightening, even when he believed that something better awaited him. His resolution repeatedly approached the point of action, only to retreat before the decisive moment.

We encounter a related struggle in the account of Henry Alline, whose religious aspirations conflicted with his enjoyment of youthful companionship and amusement. He would resolve to abandon these diversions, only to return when their attractions became immediate once more. His remorse was genuine, as was his renewed participation. Neither feeling entirely displaced the other.

These examples reveal that the strength of an ideal cannot be measured solely by the sincerity with which we acknowledge it. An aspiration may possess great intellectual and moral authority while lacking the emotional force necessary to direct our actions. Meanwhile, an established habit may continue to govern conduct long after we have ceased to approve of it.

The divided will is therefore more complicated than a simple opposition between knowledge and ignorance. It involves competing centers of motivation within the same personality, each exercising its influence under different circumstances.

To understand spiritual transformation, we must eventually discover how an ideal that remains powerless at the margins of consciousness can acquire sufficient vitality to become the governing purpose of a life.
