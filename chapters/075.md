# Day 75 — The Experience of Being Carried

*There are moments when the deepest transformation seems to occur not through the exercise of our will, but through an experience that carries us beyond its familiar boundaries.*

We ordinarily understand ourselves as active participants in the direction of our lives. We make decisions, pursue intentions, and attempt to influence our circumstances through deliberate effort. Even our inner experiences often seem subject to some degree of control, as we choose where to direct our attention and how to respond to the thoughts and feelings that arise within us.

Yet mystical consciousness frequently introduces an extraordinary reversal of this familiar relationship. Instead of experiencing themselves as the authors of what is happening, individuals report a sense of being acted upon. Their ordinary powers of choice appear temporarily suspended, while the experience unfolds with an independence that seems to exceed their personal will.

This quality may be called *passivity*, the fourth characteristic commonly associated with mystical states. The individual may prepare for such an experience through prayer, meditation, concentrated attention, or other deliberate activities. Yet when the mystical condition itself emerges, it is often perceived as something received rather than produced.

The distinction between preparation and experience is significant. We may arrange the circumstances under which an unusual state of consciousness becomes more likely, but this does not mean we can determine its precise content or summon it at will. The mystic may therefore understand the experience as an encounter with a power greater than the ordinary self.

Within religious traditions, this sense of passivity has frequently been interpreted as evidence of divine action. Individuals describe themselves as being seized, guided, illuminated, or carried by a presence that seems to possess its own intelligence and purpose. Their experience is not merely one of surrendering control, but of discovering that something else has apparently assumed it.

Psychologically, however, we must distinguish the feeling of external influence from the explanation offered for its origin. Similar alterations in the sense of personal agency occur in other unusual conditions of consciousness, including certain trances and experiences of automatic speech or writing. The impression that an experience originates beyond the conscious will does not, by itself, establish whether its source is divine, psychological, or something else.

Nevertheless, mystical states possess an important distinction from experiences that merely interrupt ordinary awareness. They generally leave behind some recollection of what occurred and a continuing sense of its significance. The individual returns to familiar consciousness carrying an experience that may influence subsequent beliefs, emotions, and decisions.

This continuity helps explain why an apparently passive event can produce lasting changes in an active life. A person may emerge from an experience feeling that familiar burdens have been lifted, that previously divided desires have become unified, or that an unexpected source of strength has become available. What seemed to occur without conscious effort may subsequently transform the direction of deliberate action.

We need not decide immediately whether such experiences reveal the operation of a supernatural power. Their psychological significance already challenges the assumption that our conscious will is the sole source of meaningful transformation.

Human beings may participate in changes whose origins remain hidden from ordinary awareness. The experience of being carried reminds us that consciousness contains possibilities of receptivity as well as effort, and that some of its most consequential developments may arrive through processes we cannot fully command.
