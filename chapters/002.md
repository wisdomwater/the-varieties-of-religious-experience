# Day 2 — Beyond the Origins of Belief

*To discover where a belief comes from is not the same as discovering whether it is true.*

Whenever we encounter an unusual religious experience, we are naturally inclined to search for its cause. We may examine the temperament of the person who experienced it, the circumstances of their upbringing, or the condition of their nervous system. Perhaps a vision arose during a period of illness, or a profound conviction emerged from a season of emotional distress. These explanations may tell us something important about how the experience came into being, but they do not necessarily tell us what the experience is worth.

Consider that every thought we entertain, whether religious or scientific, arises through the workings of the human mind and body. The convictions of the skeptic are no less dependent upon physical processes than those of the believer. If we dismiss a religious insight merely because we can identify its psychological origins, we must be prepared to dismiss every other form of human understanding on similar grounds.

We must therefore distinguish between two different questions. The first concerns the origin of an experience: How did it arise, and what conditions made it possible? The second concerns its meaning and value: Does it illuminate some aspect of reality? Does it withstand careful examination? Does it contribute something worthwhile to human life?

These questions belong together, but neither can simply replace the other. An idea born in suffering may contain genuine wisdom, just as a conviction formed in happiness may prove mistaken. The circumstances of its birth do not settle the matter.

If we wish to understand religious experience fairly, we must resist the temptation to explain it away before we have considered what it might reveal. The origins of a belief are part of its story, but they are not the final judgment upon its truth.
