# Day 80 — The Experience of Divine Union

*In the deepest expressions of mystical consciousness, the distance between the individual and the divine seems to disappear, leaving an awareness of belonging that ordinary thought can scarcely comprehend.*

Much of religious life is organized around the relationship between humanity and the divine. We imagine ourselves as finite beings seeking communion with an infinite reality, approaching something greater through prayer, contemplation, and devotion. Yet the highest forms of mystical experience often seem to transform this relationship entirely. What had previously been experienced as a distance between the seeker and the sought becomes, for a brief period, an overwhelming awareness of union.

The Christian mystic Teresa of Ávila described such an experience through what she called the *prayer of union*. In this state, the soul becomes completely absorbed in God while losing its ordinary awareness of the surrounding world. The senses no longer occupy attention, and the familiar activity of reasoning seems suspended. Even the effort to understand what is occurring becomes impossible, as consciousness is directed toward an experience that appears to exceed its ordinary faculties.

Teresa emphasized that this condition is temporary. The individual eventually returns to ordinary awareness, yet something extraordinary remains. Although the experience itself cannot be adequately examined while it is occurring, the person emerges with a profound certainty that genuine communion with God has taken place. This conviction may endure for years, even when the original state never returns.

The significance of Teresa's account lies partly in the distinction between experience and subsequent understanding. During the union itself, ordinary thought is largely inactive. Only afterward does the individual attempt to interpret what occurred, discovering a certainty that seems independent of logical demonstration. The experience is remembered not primarily as an intellectual conclusion, but as an encounter whose reality appears immediately evident to the person who underwent it.

John of the Cross described a related condition through the language of spiritual intimacy. In his account of the *union of love*, the soul receives a form of understanding that cannot be represented through ordinary images or concepts. He compares this state to an immense solitude in which the usual distractions of existence fall away, leaving the individual immersed in a mysterious awareness of divine wisdom and love.

Yet mystical union is not confined to Christian experience. The philosopher Plotinus described an encounter in which the distinction between the observer and the ultimate reality disappears. The individual no longer perceives the divine as an object standing apart from consciousness, but experiences a unity in which the familiar separation between knower and known seems overcome.

These accounts reveal a recurring feature of mystical experience, although their theological interpretations differ. For some traditions, union signifies an intimate relationship between realities that remain distinct. For others, it suggests that the apparent division between individual consciousness and ultimate reality was never fundamental. The psychological similarity of the experiences does not require us to overlook these important philosophical differences.

We must also acknowledge the difficulty of evaluating such claims from outside the experience itself. A profound conviction of divine union does not necessarily establish the metaphysical truth of the interpretation attached to it. Nevertheless, the experience possesses genuine psychological significance, particularly when it produces enduring changes in the individual's understanding of life.

Perhaps the most remarkable aspect of mystical union is the transformation it introduces into the ordinary experience of separation. The individual who once approached the divine as something distant may come to understand existence through a renewed sense of intimacy and participation. What had previously seemed like a relationship requiring continual effort becomes, at least momentarily, an immediate reality.

Whether such experiences reveal an actual union with the divine or an extraordinary possibility within human consciousness remains an open question. Yet their persistence across religious traditions suggests that the human mind is capable of encountering a form of awareness in which its most familiar boundaries lose their ordinary authority. In that experience, the distinction between seeking and finding may itself seem to disappear.
