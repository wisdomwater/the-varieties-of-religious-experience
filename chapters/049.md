# Day 49 — The Mystery of Self-Surrender

*There are moments when the effort to transform ourselves becomes the very obstacle that prevents transformation from occurring.*

Among the most remarkable features of religious conversion is the experience of self-surrender. We ordinarily assume that meaningful change requires determination, discipline, and the deliberate exercise of personal will. When we recognize something undesirable within ourselves, our natural response is to struggle against it. Yet the histories of religious transformation reveal occasions when increasing effort produces only greater frustration, and relief arrives precisely when the individual ceases trying to compel it.

The psychologist Edwin Starbuck distinguished between two general forms of conversion. In the first, the individual gradually builds a new character through conscious effort and the development of habits. In the second, transformation occurs through an apparent surrender of personal control. The individual experiences the change as something received rather than accomplished, as though forces beyond the ordinary will have assumed responsibility for what the will itself could not achieve.

This distinction is less absolute than it first appears. Even those who transform themselves through sustained effort may eventually reach a point where further striving becomes ineffective. Their conscious intentions have carried them toward an ideal, but the final movement into a new manner of life seems to require something different from the continued exertion of those intentions.

Starbuck suggests that the difficulty arises partly because deliberate striving keeps our attention fixed upon the very self we are attempting to overcome. The individual remains preoccupied with his shortcomings, continually measuring the distance between his present condition and the life he desires. Even his efforts toward improvement may reinforce the consciousness of inadequacy that has become so painful.

Self-surrender introduces another possibility. Instead of continuing to direct every movement of the transformation, the person yields to an ideal or power that has already begun to acquire influence within his life. What previously seemed an external standard to be attained becomes an organizing reality from which new feelings and actions may emerge.

Religious traditions have understood this experience as an encounter with divine grace. Psychology may describe it through the activity of subconscious processes that continue beyond deliberate awareness. These interpretations need not be hastily declared either identical or incompatible, for each approaches the experience from a different standpoint.

The mystery lies in the curious relationship between effort and release. Conscious striving may prepare the conditions for transformation, while the transformation itself arrives only when the individual relinquishes the attempt to control its completion.

Self-surrender, in this sense, is not necessarily the abandonment of aspiration. It may be the moment when aspiration ceases to revolve around the limitations of the present self and becomes sufficiently alive to carry the personality into a different way of being.
