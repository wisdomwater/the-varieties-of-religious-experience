# Day 9 — The Power of Solemn Wonder

*To encounter the sacred is to recognize that existence deserves something deeper than indifference.*

There is a particular quality of feeling that seems to accompany our most profound religious experiences. It is not simply happiness, fear, or admiration, though any of these emotions may be present. Rather, it is a sense of solemnity, an awareness that we stand before something of great significance, something that calls forth our deepest attention and cannot adequately be met with either careless amusement or bitter complaint.

Consider the many ways we respond to the circumstances of life. We may accept our existence reluctantly, regarding its difficulties as burdens that must simply be endured. We may also approach the universe with philosophical resignation, acknowledging that what happens belongs to a larger order over which we possess little control. Such acceptance may be admirable, but it does not necessarily possess the distinctive warmth of religious feeling.

Compare the measured resignation of the Stoic emperor Marcus Aurelius with the passionate surrender expressed in Christian mystical writings. Both accept their place within the universe, yet their inward attitudes differ considerably. One consents to necessity with dignity, while the other embraces what is perceived as divine goodness with affection and trust. The outward circumstances may remain unchanged, but the experience of living within them becomes profoundly different.

It is this quality of wholehearted participation that interests me. In its fullest expression, religion does more than persuade us to endure existence. It awakens a willingness to embrace life with reverence, even when life contains sorrow. Its joy is not shallow amusement, for it recognizes the gravity of suffering. Its sorrow is not mere despair, for it remains open to the possibility of deliverance.

Solemnity, then, is not the absence of happiness, but a deepening of it. It is the recognition that our existence carries a significance worthy of both tenderness and awe. Through such an attitude, the familiar world may become charged with a meaning that ordinary acceptance alone cannot provide.
