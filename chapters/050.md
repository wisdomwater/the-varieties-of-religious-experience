# Day 50 — The Limits of Willpower

*We may command our actions through determination, but the deepest movements of the heart do not always submit to our instructions.*

The human will is a remarkable instrument. Through determination, we can discipline our habits, resist immediate desires, and undertake difficult courses of action. Much of what we admire in moral character depends upon this capacity to direct ourselves toward purposes that require sustained effort. Yet there are limits to what determination can accomplish, particularly when the change we desire concerns the fundamental condition of our inner lives.

Consider a person overwhelmed by anxiety, guilt, or despair. We may advise him to abandon his fears, embrace hope, and believe that his life possesses meaning. Such counsel may be perfectly reasonable from our own standpoint, yet to the suffering individual it can sound almost absurd. His immediate experience assures him that something is terribly wrong, and no amount of deliberate insistence can necessarily make the opposite conviction feel true.

We may strengthen a belief whose foundations already exist within us, but we cannot simply manufacture a living conviction whenever we desire one. The will can direct attention toward an ideal, yet it cannot always supply the emotional assurance that would make the ideal a reality within consciousness.

This difficulty becomes particularly evident in religious conversion. The individual may struggle continually to overcome feelings of spiritual inadequacy, examining every thought and action for evidence of improvement. Yet the more intensely he strives, the more firmly his attention may remain fixed upon his supposed deficiencies. His determination keeps alive the very consciousness from which he seeks deliverance.

The American revivalist Charles Finney described a curious experience following his own spiritual crisis. When the burden of religious anxiety unexpectedly disappeared, he became concerned that his lack of distress might indicate the withdrawal of divine influence. He attempted to recover his former anxiety, but discovered that he could no longer produce it through deliberate effort.

The incident illustrates how little direct command we sometimes possess over our deepest emotional states. We may wish to feel remorse and discover only peace, just as we may desperately desire peace while remaining unable to escape our fears.

None of this renders the will unimportant. Deliberate effort can shape conduct, establish habits, and prepare the conditions under which new attitudes may emerge. But we must distinguish the ability to pursue a transformation from the ability to produce every element of that transformation by command.

The limits of willpower reveal something essential about human nature. We are capable of choosing many of our actions, yet the convictions and affections from which our actions arise belong to a mental life more extensive than conscious determination alone. Understanding this distinction may help explain why some of the most consequential changes occur precisely when our efforts have reached their limit.
