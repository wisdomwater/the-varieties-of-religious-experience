# Day 67 — The Dangers of Spiritual Excess

*Even our noblest qualities can become distorted when they grow so powerful that they leave no room for the other dimensions of a fully human life.*

We naturally admire individuals whose convictions are sufficiently strong to govern their entire existence. Their devotion, purity, and willingness to sacrifice may appear as extraordinary achievements of human character. Yet admiration becomes more complicated when these qualities develop without the balancing influence of practical judgment, intellectual breadth, and ordinary human sympathy. What begins as spiritual excellence may gradually become a form of narrowness that diminishes rather than enlarges the possibilities of life.

This difficulty is not unique to religion. Great artists, reformers, and thinkers have sometimes pursued their particular callings with such intensity that other valuable concerns were neglected. Their achievements may remain worthy of admiration, even while their manner of living would be undesirable as a universal example. We may appreciate what their unusual concentration accomplished without concluding that everyone ought to imitate their entire character.

Religious devotion presents similar possibilities. When spiritual feeling becomes overwhelmingly powerful in a person whose interests and understanding are limited, the individual may lose the capacity to participate meaningfully in ordinary human affairs. I have described one form of this condition as *theopathic*, a state in which absorption in the love of God displaces nearly every other concern.

Margaret Mary Alacoque provides a revealing example. Her religious experiences were accompanied by extraordinary tenderness, humility, and devotion, yet accounts of her convent life suggest that she struggled with practical responsibilities. Attempts to employ her in the kitchen were unsuccessful because of her clumsiness, while her absorption in spiritual matters interfered with her ability to supervise children in the schoolroom. The very intensity that made her an object of religious admiration also limited her usefulness in the daily life of her community.

We encounter another kind of excess in the pursuit of purity. Individuals who become intensely sensitive to moral inconsistency may gradually withdraw from relationships, occupations, and ordinary pleasures that seem capable of disturbing their spiritual condition. The desire for inward harmony can become so demanding that life must be continually restricted to preserve it.

These examples suggest that the problem may lie less in possessing too much of a particular virtue than in possessing too little of the qualities necessary to guide it. Strong affection benefits from intellectual understanding, just as strong conviction requires sympathy and judgment. Where these capacities develop together, they may reinforce one another. Where they become separated, even admirable impulses can produce unfortunate consequences.

We must therefore distinguish spiritual intensity from spiritual maturity. The individual who experiences extraordinary devotion may nevertheless remain limited in understanding, practical ability, or concern for others. Such limitations need not invalidate the sincerity of the experience, but they do affect our judgment of its value.

The most complete form of human excellence may require a harmony among qualities that are often cultivated separately. A life rich in spiritual feeling becomes more admirable when its tenderness is accompanied by wisdom, its purity by generosity, and its devotion by an intelligent concern for the world in which other people must live.

The question is not how far a single virtue can be intensified, but whether the whole person has become more capable of participating wisely and generously in human existence.
