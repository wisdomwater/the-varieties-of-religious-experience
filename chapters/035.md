# Day 35 — The Many Selves Within Us

*Within a single human personality, different tendencies may struggle for expression, each claiming to represent who we truly are.*

We are accustomed to thinking of ourselves as unified beings, possessing a recognizable character, a consistent set of values, and a will capable of directing our actions. Yet careful observation reveals that human personality is often far less harmonious than this familiar picture suggests. Within the same individual, impulses may arise that seem almost to belong to different people. We may sincerely desire one thing while feeling ourselves powerfully drawn toward its opposite.

The French novelist Alphonse Daudet described a striking experience of this inner duality. When his brother died, one part of him was overcome with grief, while another observed the dramatic quality of his father's expression of sorrow, considering how effective it might appear upon a theatrical stage. He was disturbed to discover that his consciousness could simultaneously participate in an experience and stand apart from it as an almost detached observer.

Such divisions need not always be so dramatic. Annie Besant, in her autobiography, described herself as remarkably courageous when defending her convictions before a public audience, yet painfully timid in ordinary personal encounters. She could withstand vigorous opposition during a debate while finding it almost unbearable to correct a servant or risk another person's disapproval. Both tendencies belonged to her character, although they seemed strangely incompatible.

Some individuals possess a naturally harmonious temperament. Their feelings cooperate with their judgments, their desires support their purposes, and their actions proceed with relatively little internal resistance. Others experience a more complicated arrangement. Their impulses compete for authority, their intentions are repeatedly interrupted, and their lives may become a continuous movement between aspiration, inconsistency, regret, and renewed effort.

The causes of these differences are not entirely clear. We must be cautious about reducing such complexity to any single explanation. What matters for our present inquiry is the psychological fact that personality does not always possess the unity we ordinarily attribute to it.

This observation becomes especially important when considering religious transformation. If the human being is already divided among conflicting tendencies, spiritual change may involve more than adopting new beliefs or correcting particular habits. It may require a reorganization of the personality itself, through which previously competing elements gradually come to serve a more coherent purpose.

Before we can understand how such unification occurs, however, we must recognize the remarkable diversity of the forces already living within us.
