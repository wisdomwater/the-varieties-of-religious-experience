# Day 44 — The Shifting Center of the Self

*What governs our lives is not always what we believe most deeply, but what has become most alive within our consciousness.*

To understand the psychology of conversion, we must examine how the interests and convictions that compose our personality acquire their influence. Our minds contain numerous groups of ideas, each associated with particular desires, emotions, and purposes. These groups do not always operate together. Instead, one may occupy our attention so completely that others temporarily disappear from practical consideration.

Consider a person who holds an important public office. While attending to official responsibilities, his thoughts are occupied with decisions, obligations, and the expectations of others. Yet when he retreats into the wilderness for a holiday, an entirely different set of interests may take possession of him. The concerns of public life recede, while the simple activities of outdoor existence become immediate and absorbing. Although the individual remains the same person, the character of his consciousness has undergone a noticeable alteration.

Ordinarily, such changes are temporary. We pass from one occupation to another, allowing different interests to govern us according to circumstance. But suppose that one particular interest should become so firmly established that it permanently displaces its former rivals. We would then recognize something more substantial than a passing change of mood. We would speak of a transformation of character.

The essential distinction lies in the difference between ideas that are merely present in consciousness and those that possess the power to direct our energies. Some convictions remain cold and inactive, acknowledged intellectually but seldom translated into conduct. Others are charged with emotional vitality. They command our attention, awaken our desires, and give direction to our will.

We may call the collection of interests from which a person habitually acts the center of his personal energy. The location of this center profoundly influences the character of his life. A religious belief may remain peripheral for years, exerting little practical authority, until an alteration in emotional interest brings it into the foreground.

I recall hearing, in my youth, about the establishment of the Gifford Lectureships. The announcement seemed entirely remote from my own future. Yet here I stand, years later, delivering one of those lectures, with an undertaking once foreign to my imagination now occupying the center of my attention and effort.

Our interests are therefore not fixed in their relative importance. What seems distant today may become intimately connected with our identity tomorrow.

In religious conversion, this ordinary capacity for shifting attention and emotional investment assumes extraordinary significance. Religious ideas cease to occupy the outskirts of consciousness and become the habitual source of personal energy. What remains to be explained is how such a profound rearrangement becomes possible, and why certain convictions suddenly acquire a vitality they previously lacked.
