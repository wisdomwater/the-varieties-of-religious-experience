# Day 33 — What Optimism Cannot Answer

*An outlook that brings peace to one person may still be too narrow to account for the whole of human experience.*

We have now examined two profoundly different ways of encountering existence. The healthy-minded individual discovers goodness almost instinctively, finding in life sufficient reasons for gratitude, confidence, and joy. The sick soul, by contrast, remains acutely conscious of suffering, limitation, and the troubling uncertainties that surround our happiness. Each temperament inhabits the same universe, yet the experiences through which that universe becomes meaningful are remarkably different.

It would be a mistake to dismiss healthy-mindedness merely because it cannot satisfy everyone. Its practical achievements are genuine. Countless individuals have discovered serenity and renewed vitality by directing their attention toward what is good, refusing to surrender their lives to unnecessary fear and regret. Within the limits of its successful operation, such an outlook deserves our respect.

Yet a difficulty emerges when we attempt to transform this personal achievement into a universal philosophy. The person who has found happiness by turning away from suffering has not thereby explained the suffering that remains. Their success demonstrates that a certain way of living is possible, but it does not establish that this way of living provides an adequate understanding of existence as a whole.

Indeed, the sick soul appears to occupy a wider field of experience. Those who suffer profoundly are generally acquainted with happiness, or at least with its memory and possibility. They know something of both the light and darkness of human consciousness. The radically healthy-minded individual, however, may remain almost entirely unfamiliar with the forms of anguish that govern another person's existence.

This does not mean that melancholy guarantees wisdom. Suffering can distort perception just as happiness can narrow it, and neither temperament possesses an unquestionable claim to truth. Nevertheless, a philosophy that includes the reality of evil possesses an advantage over one that refuses to consider it.

Our task is therefore not to exchange optimism for pessimism, but to seek an understanding large enough to acknowledge the evidence offered by both.

If religious experience is to illuminate the human condition, it must have something meaningful to say not only to those who rejoice in existence, but also to those who have discovered how terribly difficult existence can become.
