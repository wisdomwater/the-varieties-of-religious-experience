# Day 54 — Becoming a New Kind of Person

*The deepest significance of conversion may lie in the discovery that our character contains possibilities we could scarcely have imagined before experiencing them.*

As we conclude our examination of conversion, we must consider what it means for a person to become genuinely different. We have encountered extraordinary experiences of religious assurance, sudden changes of conviction, and transformations that emerge through gradual development. Yet the most important question remains whether these experiences produce a lasting alteration in the character of the individual.

Human personality possesses a remarkable capacity for reorganization. Desires that once governed our conduct may lose their influence, while aspirations previously regarded as distant or impossible acquire the power to direct our lives. The individual may discover new courage, patience, or generosity, not merely through a greater determination to behave differently, but because the emotional foundations of behavior have themselves changed.

Consider the difference between resisting a temptation and no longer experiencing it with the same intensity. In the first case, the undesirable impulse remains powerful, and moral conduct depends upon continual opposition. In the second, something more fundamental has altered. A competing affection or purpose has become so influential that the former temptation no longer occupies its previous position within the personality.

Religious conversion sometimes produces changes of this kind. Yet we must not assume that every convert becomes permanently free from conflicting desires. Edwin Starbuck's investigations suggest that individuals frequently experience fluctuations in religious enthusiasm while retaining a relatively stable commitment to their new spiritual orientation.

This distinction is important because transformation need not imply an uninterrupted condition of emotional exaltation. A person may encounter discouragement, uncertainty, and occasional failures without returning entirely to the former organization of life. The convictions that now shape his identity may endure even when the feelings accompanying their original discovery have diminished.

Nor should we regard the converted personality as an entirely separate species of human being. The same capacities for courage, devotion, and self-transcendence exist in varying degrees throughout humanity. What distinguishes the transformed individual is the unusual strength and stability with which certain possibilities have become realized.

We have therefore arrived at a new stage of our inquiry. Having examined how religious experience may reorganize the inner life, we must now investigate the kind of character that sometimes emerges from this process.

Our attention must turn from the experience of becoming to the qualities of the person who has become. What happens when religious conviction does more than provide comfort or assurance, and begins to shape an entire manner of living?

It is this question that leads us into the study of saintliness, where the value of religious experience must be examined through its most enduring expressions in human character.
