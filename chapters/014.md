# Day 14 — The Once-Born Soul

*Not every soul must pass through darkness to discover the goodness of existence.*

There is a type of religious temperament that seems to arrive at spiritual confidence without passing through any great crisis of despair or conversion. Such individuals do not experience themselves as fundamentally estranged from the divine, nor do they feel that their lives must be broken apart and reconstructed before they can discover peace. Their faith develops naturally, as though the relationship between themselves and the sacred had always been present, awaiting only greater understanding.

Francis William Newman described such individuals as the once-born. They tend to perceive God not primarily as a stern judge demanding satisfaction, but as the benevolent spirit of a beautiful and harmonious world. Their awareness of personal imperfection seldom becomes a source of torment. They may recognize their faults and seek to correct them, yet they do not regard their entire existence as corrupted by sin.

Consider Edward Everett Hale, the Unitarian minister who described growing up in a home where religion was presented as simple, reasonable, and full of affection. He could scarcely understand the spiritual struggles that occupied so many religious biographies. From childhood, he had understood himself to be loved by God, and this confidence made learning, serving others, and participating in life seem like natural expressions of gratitude.

Such a temperament challenges the assumption that profound suffering must always precede spiritual maturity. We sometimes imagine that a person must first experience guilt, despair, or alienation before discovering the divine. Yet the once-born soul reminds us that religion may also grow through an uninterrupted deepening of trust.

I do not suggest that this path provides a complete understanding of human existence. Its very freedom from inner conflict may leave certain dimensions of suffering unexplored. But neither should we dismiss its sincerity simply because it lacks the drama of a spiritual crisis.

The once-born soul reveals one of religion's gentler possibilities: a life in which faith develops not through rescue from despair, but through the steady unfolding of an affection that was present from the beginning.
