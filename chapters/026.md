# Day 26 — When Success Loses Its Meaning

*The achievement of everything we desire may still leave unanswered the question of what makes life worth living.*

We often imagine that unhappiness arises from the distance between our aspirations and our accomplishments. If we could overcome our limitations, fulfill our ambitions, and secure the admiration of others, surely we would discover the satisfaction that has so often escaped us. Yet human experience presents a troubling contradiction. Some of those who have achieved the greatest success have also confessed to a profound inward sense of failure.

Consider Goethe, whose literary accomplishments and extraordinary influence might seem to represent a life richly fulfilled. Yet in his later years, he described his existence as an almost continuous burden, comparing his efforts to the endless labor of pushing a stone uphill. Martin Luther, whose religious achievements transformed the course of European history, likewise spoke in old age of his weariness with life and his longing for its conclusion.

These testimonies challenge our ordinary understanding of success. The judgments of the world and the judgments of our inward consciousness do not necessarily agree. A person may attain everything others regard as desirable while remaining painfully aware of unfulfilled ideals, neglected possibilities, or failures concealed from public view. Indeed, our aspirations may grow alongside our achievements, so that each accomplishment reveals new distances between what we are and what we believe we ought to become.

There is also a deeper difficulty. Even when our ambitions are fulfilled, success cannot free us from the conditions of mortal existence. The most celebrated achievements remain vulnerable to time, and the individual who possesses them must eventually surrender everything that has been gained.

We should not conclude that achievement is meaningless or that human effort is futile. Such a judgment would overlook the genuine goods that accomplishment brings. But we must recognize that outward success cannot guarantee inward peace, nor can the approval of others finally settle our understanding of our own worth.

Perhaps this explains why the experience of failure occupies such an important place in religious consciousness. When the ordinary measures of a successful life prove insufficient, the individual may begin searching for a deeper standard of value.

The question then becomes not merely whether we have succeeded in life, but whether success itself can answer our longing for lasting meaning.
