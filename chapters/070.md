# Day 70 — By Their Fruits We Shall Know Them

*The deepest significance of a spiritual life may be revealed less by the beliefs it professes than by the kind of person those beliefs help to create.*

We have now examined the principal characteristics of saintliness, together with the difficulties that arise when its virtues develop beyond the guidance of sound judgment. We have encountered extraordinary courage, compassion, generosity, purity, and devotion, but also fanaticism, excessive self-denial, and withdrawal from ordinary human responsibilities. Our task is to determine what these varied expressions of religious character reveal about the value of religion itself.

If we approached this question with a predetermined theological system, our judgment might appear relatively simple. We could measure each individual against an established conception of spiritual perfection, approving whatever conformed to that standard and rejecting whatever did not. Yet such a method would assume precisely what our investigation seeks to understand. We must instead examine religious life as a human phenomenon whose significance becomes visible through experience.

The saintly character offers substantial evidence in religion's favor. Those deeply conscious of a divine order frequently discover a source of happiness that ordinary misfortune cannot easily destroy. Their convictions may release them from the tyranny of personal ambition, diminish their fear of suffering, and inspire an extraordinary willingness to serve others. At their best, such individuals become sources of strength and encouragement for everyone they encounter.

These qualities are not confined exclusively to religious people. Courage, kindness, patience, and self-discipline may also arise from temperament, philosophical conviction, or devotion to humanity. Yet their remarkable combination within the saintly personality suggests that religious experience can organize the whole character around an unusually powerful center of meaning.

We must nevertheless acknowledge that saintliness does not guarantee wisdom. Spiritual enthusiasm may reinforce narrow beliefs as readily as generous ones, and an individual's sincerity provides no assurance that his actions will benefit others. Our judgment must therefore consider the relationship between intention, conduct, and consequences, rather than treating religious fervor as an achievement sufficient in itself.

Nor can we expect every person to embody the same ideal in precisely the same way. Human capacities and circumstances differ, and the form of religious life appropriate to one individual may be unsuitable for another. Some are called toward contemplation, others toward active service, and still others toward forms of courage and reform that require engagement with the world's conflicts.

Our conclusions must consequently remain qualified. On the whole, the finest expressions of saintliness represent some of the most valuable achievements of human nature. Their tenderness, courage, and devotion enlarge our imagination of what a human life can become, even when their particular practices cannot reasonably be recommended for universal imitation.

Yet an important question remains unanswered. If religious convictions produce admirable consequences, does that establish the truth of the unseen reality toward which they point? A belief may transform an individual's character without necessarily providing an accurate description of the universe.

We have good reason to take religious experience seriously because of what it accomplishes in human lives. Whether its transformative power also reveals something objectively true about the ultimate nature of existence requires a further investigation.

It is toward this question that we must now turn, examining those extraordinary states of consciousness in which individuals claim to encounter reality more directly than ordinary understanding seems to permit.
