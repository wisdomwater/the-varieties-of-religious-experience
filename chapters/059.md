# Day 59 — Freedom from the Tyranny of Fear

*True courage may emerge when the things we fear lose their power to determine how we live.*

Fear is among the most powerful influences upon human conduct. We fear suffering, rejection, humiliation, poverty, and death, and much of our behavior is organized around avoiding these possibilities. Such caution is not necessarily unreasonable, for fear serves an important protective function. Yet when it becomes the governing principle of existence, our lives may gradually narrow until the preservation of security takes precedence over nearly every other aspiration.

One of the remarkable characteristics of saintliness is the apparent weakening of this authority. Individuals whose lives are deeply rooted in religious conviction sometimes display an extraordinary indifference toward circumstances that would ordinarily inspire terror. They may face imprisonment, persecution, illness, or even death with a composure that appears almost beyond ordinary human possibility.

The French mystic Madame Guyon provides a striking illustration. Although physically frail, she described encountering serious dangers with an unusual serenity. When she was imprisoned for her religious beliefs, her friends wept over her circumstances, yet she reported experiencing a profound acceptance of what had happened. Her confidence in divine providence had become sufficiently powerful that concern for her personal welfare no longer dominated her emotional life.

On another occasion, Guyon recounted a dangerous river crossing during which her carriage became trapped in quicksand. Those accompanying her struggled to escape in terror, while she remained absorbed in thoughts of God. She recognized the possibility of drowning, yet reported feeling remarkably little distress at the prospect.

Such accounts may seem almost incomprehensible to those of us who ordinarily experience fear as an involuntary response to danger. Yet their psychological significance becomes clearer when we consider the relationship between fear and attachment. Much of our anxiety arises from the possibility of losing something we regard as essential to our happiness or identity. When the center of personal meaning shifts toward a reality believed to be beyond the reach of ordinary misfortune, certain fears may lose their customary force.

This does not mean that religious courage should be confused with recklessness. Fear can alert us to genuine dangers, and indifference toward personal safety is not necessarily evidence of wisdom. We must distinguish the ability to endure unavoidable suffering from the unnecessary pursuit or neglect of danger.

Nevertheless, these experiences reveal how profoundly our understanding of existence can influence our capacity for courage. When individuals cease to regard personal security as the highest good, possibilities of action emerge that fear might otherwise prevent.

The freedom displayed in such lives is therefore not simply the absence of apprehension. It is the discovery of a purpose or confidence strong enough that fear no longer possesses the final authority over what a person is willing to become.
