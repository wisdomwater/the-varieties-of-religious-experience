# Day 58 — The Peace That Changes Everything

*The deepest peace may not arise from the disappearance of life's difficulties, but from a change in our relationship to everything we cannot control.*

Among the most remarkable fruits of religious experience is a condition of inward tranquillity that seems capable of enduring circumstances ordinarily associated with fear and distress. Those who possess it may encounter suffering, uncertainty, and danger without the usual degree of emotional disturbance. Their outward circumstances remain subject to the same difficulties that confront everyone, yet something within their understanding of existence has become profoundly settled.

We can appreciate the psychological basis of this condition when we consider what it means to believe that our lives are held within the care of a power worthy of absolute trust. Much of human anxiety arises from our awareness of vulnerability and our inability to control the future. If an individual becomes deeply convinced that life possesses an underlying security, the emotional significance of those uncertainties may be considerably diminished.

This confidence does not assume precisely the same character in every temperament. In naturally cheerful individuals, it may appear as a joyful acceptance of whatever circumstances arise. In those disposed toward melancholy, it may express itself through a quieter resignation, a willingness to endure suffering without demanding that life conform to personal expectations.

The French philosopher Jules Lagneau provides an example of the more somber disposition. Although burdened by serious illness, he wrote of seeking the courage to continue without insisting upon deliverance from his condition. His words reveal a stern kind of peace, sustained not by optimism concerning his circumstances, but by an attitude of acceptance toward what life might bring.

Blaise Pascal expressed a related spirit in his prayers during illness. Rather than asking unconditionally for health or relief, he sought to bring his own desires into harmony with what he understood as divine providence. The central concern was no longer whether life would unfold pleasantly, but whether he could remain faithful within whatever circumstances occurred.

Such experiences present us with an important distinction between happiness and peace. Happiness commonly depends upon the presence of agreeable conditions, while the tranquillity described here may persist even when those conditions are absent. It represents an alteration in the emotional authority that suffering and uncertainty exercise over the individual.

We should not romanticize suffering or suppose that genuine spiritual maturity requires indifference toward pain. Nor does inward peace necessarily imply passivity in the face of circumstances that might reasonably be changed.

Nevertheless, these accounts reveal an extraordinary human possibility. Our experience of the world depends not only upon the events that happen to us, but also upon the larger understanding through which we receive them. When that understanding becomes rooted in a profound sense of trust, even an uncertain life may be inhabited with a steadiness that ordinary calculations of security cannot provide.
