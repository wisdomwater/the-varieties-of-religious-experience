# Day 47 — The Hidden Work of the Subconscious

*The boundaries of our awareness are not necessarily the boundaries of the activity taking place within us.*

We commonly regard the conscious will as the principal author of our decisions and transformations. We deliberate, choose a course of action, and attempt to direct our thoughts toward a desired result. Yet ordinary experience suggests that the mind accomplishes many things without our continuous supervision. Some of its most important developments appear to take place beyond the immediate reach of deliberate attention.

Consider the familiar difficulty of trying to remember a forgotten name. We search through associated memories, examine possible connections, and make repeated efforts to recover the missing word. Sometimes these efforts accomplish nothing. We finally abandon the search and turn our attention elsewhere, only to discover, some time later, that the forgotten name has returned spontaneously.

It appears that a process initiated by conscious effort may continue after that effort has ceased. Something within the mind remains occupied with the problem, although we are no longer deliberately attending to it. The eventual result enters consciousness almost as if it had arrived from an independent source.

Psychologists have attempted to describe such occurrences through the language of unconscious or subconscious mental activity. The precise nature of these processes remains difficult to establish, but their existence raises an important possibility for our understanding of religious transformation. Convictions, emotional tendencies, and aspirations may be undergoing development outside the narrow region of consciousness to which we ordinarily have access.

Edwin Starbuck's investigations suggest that the gradual formation of a new religious personality can involve just such processes. Conscious effort may initiate a movement toward change, while influences not fully accessible to awareness contribute to its eventual completion. The person who experiences the result may therefore be unable to explain precisely how it occurred.

We should avoid assuming that this psychological explanation settles every question concerning conversion. To describe a transformation as arising through subconscious activity does not prove that we have identified all the forces involved, nor does it determine whether the experience possesses a significance beyond its psychological mechanism.

Nevertheless, the distinction between conscious intention and subconscious development deserves careful attention. It challenges the assumption that every meaningful change must be produced through continuous deliberate effort.

Human consciousness may be only the visible portion of a more extensive mental life. If so, the emergence of a new conviction or disposition may reveal the results of an inward development that has been proceeding beyond our immediate awareness. What appears to us as a sudden discovery may be the first conscious recognition of something long in preparation.
