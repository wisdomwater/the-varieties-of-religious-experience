# Day 53 — The Difference Between Feeling and Transformation

*The significance of a spiritual experience cannot be measured by the intensity of the moment alone, but must also be considered in the light of the life that follows.*

Religious conversion sometimes arrives accompanied by extraordinary emotional intensity. Individuals describe overwhelming happiness, luminous visions, an irresistible sense of divine presence, or an assurance that seems to settle every question concerning their spiritual condition. Such experiences may become the defining memories of an entire lifetime. Yet we must ask whether their dramatic character necessarily indicates a correspondingly profound transformation of personality.

It is tempting to assume that the more extraordinary an experience appears, the greater its spiritual significance must be. Certain religious traditions have placed particular emphasis upon instantaneous conversion, regarding its suddenness as evidence of divine intervention. But psychological observation does not justify the conclusion that those who undergo dramatic transformations are necessarily more spiritually developed than those whose character changes gradually.

The psychologist George Coe investigated seventy-seven individuals who had experienced conversion or sought it. His findings suggested that striking conversions were associated with particular psychological dispositions, including emotional sensitivity, susceptibility to suggestion, and unusual forms of subconscious activity. These characteristics might help explain why some individuals experience profound changes dramatically, while others undergo comparable developments without extraordinary sensations.

Such an explanation need not diminish the significance of the experience itself. The origins and mechanisms of a transformation are distinct from its value. If an experience produces greater compassion, moral courage, generosity, or stability of character, its importance remains worthy of recognition regardless of whether its psychological development can be explained.

We should also distinguish the persistence of religious conviction from the persistence of religious emotion. Starbuck's investigation of one hundred converts revealed that many reported periods of spiritual decline or diminished enthusiasm. Yet only a small minority described abandoning the religious faith associated with their conversion. The intensity of feeling could fluctuate considerably while the underlying orientation of life remained relatively stable.

This observation complicates any simple division between genuine and unsuccessful conversion. An individual may lose the excitement of an original awakening without losing its lasting influence upon character. Conversely, powerful emotion does not automatically establish the moral excellence of the person who experiences it.

We must therefore resist two opposite errors: dismissing extraordinary religious experiences merely because they possess recognizable psychological characteristics, and accepting them as spiritually superior merely because they are extraordinary.

Their importance lies partly in the possibilities of human consciousness that they reveal, and partly in the qualities of life that emerge from them. The experience deserves to be understood on its own terms, while its enduring value must be considered through the character, relationships, and conduct of the person who has undergone it.
