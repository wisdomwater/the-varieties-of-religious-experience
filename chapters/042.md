# Day 42 — Finding a New Center of Gravity

*The deepest transformations of character occur when something once peripheral to our lives becomes the center around which everything else takes its place.*

As we conclude our examination of the divided self, we arrive at a remarkable feature of human consciousness. Our personality is not merely a collection of beliefs, feelings, and desires. These elements exist in a changing relationship with one another, some occupying positions of commanding importance while others remain at the margins of attention. What governs our lives depends not only upon what we believe or desire, but upon which of these tendencies possesses the greatest influence over our conduct.

We may understand this arrangement through the image of a center of gravity. When the personality is divided, competing interests struggle to occupy its center. One aspiration commands our loyalty in moments of reflection, while another governs our actions when circumstances change. The resulting instability can produce considerable suffering, for we find ourselves repeatedly abandoning purposes that we sincerely regard as worthy.

Yet this arrangement is capable of transformation. A conviction that once seemed distant may gradually become the organizing principle of our existence. An attachment that previously absorbed our attention may lose its importance, while an entirely different understanding of life acquires the power to direct our energies. When such a reorganization becomes sufficiently stable, we recognize that something fundamental has changed in the individual.

The experiences of Tolstoy and Bunyan illustrate this movement in different ways. Tolstoy discovered renewed vitality through a conception of life rooted in faith and moral purpose, while Bunyan gradually found assurance in the grace of Christ. Neither man became indifferent to the suffering and uncertainty that had previously troubled him. Rather, each discovered a source of meaning that altered the place those difficulties occupied within his consciousness.

This is an essential distinction. The unification of personality does not necessarily require the removal of every painful feeling or conflicting impulse. What changes is the relationship among them. The experiences that once dominated the entire horizon of consciousness may remain present, but they no longer possess the same authority over the direction of life.

We have now seen that this transformation may occur gradually or suddenly, through religious experience or through other powerful influences. Its psychological form is recognizable even when its ultimate meaning remains open to interpretation.

Our next task is to examine this process more closely, particularly in the religious experience traditionally called conversion. We must ask how a previously divided personality comes to find a new center, what forces make such a change possible, and why the resulting transformation can feel like the beginning of another life.
