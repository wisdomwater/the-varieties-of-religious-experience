# Day 39 — The Longing to Become Whole

*Beneath our conflicting desires lies a longing for a life in which our energies can move together toward a common purpose.*

As we have examined the divided personality, we have encountered individuals whose lives seem governed by competing forces. Their convictions lead them in one direction, their habits in another, while their emotions and affections refuse to cooperate with either. Such people may possess intelligence, sincerity, and considerable strength of character, yet remain unable to establish a lasting peace within themselves. Their suffering arises from the exhausting experience of being continually pulled between incompatible possibilities.

This condition reveals something important about the nature of happiness. We ordinarily associate happiness with pleasant experiences, successful circumstances, or the satisfaction of our desires. Yet an individual may possess all these things and remain profoundly unhappy if the different elements of his personality are in conflict. Conversely, a person whose outward circumstances are difficult may discover remarkable contentment when his inner life becomes organized around a purpose he can wholeheartedly embrace.

What we seek in such moments is a kind of inward unity. We desire an arrangement of our feelings, convictions, and actions in which one tendency no longer continually undermines another. The mind longs for stability, for some enduring orientation through which its various energies may find a more consistent expression.

Religion has often provided the conditions for such a transformation. Through faith, devotion, or an overwhelming experience of spiritual assurance, individuals have discovered a new relationship among the competing elements of their consciousness. What formerly seemed an intolerable struggle may give way to a sense of settled purpose. The relief can be so profound that those who experience it describe themselves as having entered an entirely new life.

Yet we must recognize that this psychological movement is not exclusively religious. Love, intellectual conviction, patriotic devotion, and even less admirable passions may organize a previously divided personality. The achievement of inner unity, considered by itself, does not establish the moral worth or ultimate truth of the purpose around which the personality becomes organized.

This distinction deserves our attention. A harmonious character may be admirable, but harmony alone cannot tell us whether the direction of a life is worthy of approval. We must eventually consider both the psychological transformation and the fruits it produces.

For now, the essential observation is that human beings possess a remarkable capacity to move beyond inward division. The longing for wholeness points toward the possibility that our competing impulses need not remain forever in their present arrangement, and that a more coherent manner of living may yet become possible.
