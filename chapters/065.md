# Day 65 — The Dignity of Every Human Being

*When we recognize a worth in others that no worldly distinction can increase or diminish, the boundaries separating human lives begin to lose their authority.*

Human societies have always established distinctions of rank, wealth, influence, and achievement. We organize our relationships around differences in power and position, granting particular forms of recognition to those who possess advantages that others lack. These distinctions may serve practical purposes, yet they can also encourage us to confuse a person's social standing with his essential worth.

One of the more remarkable consequences of religious experience is the possibility of seeing through these conventional hierarchies. When individuals become deeply conscious of their relationship to a divine reality, the distinctions that ordinarily govern social life may appear strangely insignificant. Kings and laborers, scholars and servants, the wealthy and the poor are understood as participants in a common existence whose deepest significance lies beyond their worldly circumstances.

This sentiment found a particularly forceful expression among the early Quakers. George Fox and his followers refused to remove their hats as gestures of deference toward social superiors, and they addressed individuals without the customary distinctions associated with rank. To outsiders, these practices appeared unnecessarily provocative. Yet for the Quakers, such conventions represented a contradiction between their professed spiritual equality and their everyday conduct.

Their convictions were costly. Fox described the insults, beatings, and imprisonment endured by those who refused to observe traditional forms of respect. What appeared to others as a trivial matter of etiquette had become, for them, a question of personal integrity and human equality.

A related sentiment appears in religious attitudes toward poverty. Those who regard every person as standing equally before God may become reluctant to claim advantages unavailable to others. The desire for wealth and distinction can begin to seem less important than participation in a common humanity.

The writer John Jay Chapman explored this possibility in reflecting upon the radical demands of Christian love. He suggested that those whose concern for humanity becomes sufficiently profound may gradually abandon luxury and personal privilege, not because deprivation is their original objective, but because the welfare of others has become more compelling than their former pursuits.

Here we encounter an important distinction between humility and the recognition of universal dignity. Humility may involve a diminished concern for our own importance, while the sentiment of humanity involves an enlarged appreciation of the importance of others. The two frequently accompany one another, but their psychological meanings are not identical.

We should recognize that spiritual convictions have not always produced social equality. Religious institutions have frequently reinforced the very hierarchies that their highest ideals might challenge. The presence of a doctrine affirming human worth does not guarantee that its adherents will embody that conviction.

Nevertheless, the saintly temperament reveals a possibility of profound social significance. When our perception of others is no longer governed primarily by status, usefulness, or similarity to ourselves, relationships can become expressions of a deeper recognition.

The dignity of another person need not be earned through achievement, established through possessions, or granted by social approval. It may instead be encountered as something already present, awaiting the transformation of perception necessary for us to recognize it.
