# Day 11 — The Presence We Cannot Explain

*There are experiences in which we feel the presence of something unseen with a certainty that exceeds our ability to explain it.*

Our ordinary understanding of reality depends heavily upon the senses. We trust what we can see, hear, and touch, and we naturally assume that these faculties provide the foundation of our knowledge. Yet there are occasions when a person becomes profoundly aware of a presence without receiving any recognizable impression through the senses. Nothing appears before their eyes, no voice reaches their ears, and no physical contact occurs. Nevertheless, the conviction that something is present may be overwhelming.

I have encountered accounts of this peculiar experience among individuals of considerable intelligence and self-awareness. One acquaintance described moments when he felt another presence enter his room, producing an extraordinary sense of certainty despite the absence of any visible or audible figure. Some of these encounters were deeply disturbing, while others filled him with an almost indescribable joy. Interestingly, he did not necessarily interpret the joyful experiences as encounters with God, although another person might readily have done so.

Such accounts suggest that human consciousness may possess a more general sense of reality than the impressions delivered by our individual senses. We appear capable of feeling that something exists even when we cannot identify what it is or explain how we have become aware of it. The experience may be psychologically real and intensely convincing, while the nature of its apparent object remains uncertain.

Religious history contains many similar testimonies, often interpreted as encounters with divine presence. Those who undergo them may find their convictions strengthened beyond anything that philosophical reasoning alone could accomplish.

We must be careful, however, not to confuse the certainty of an experience with certainty about its cause. The feeling of presence deserves serious examination, but its intensity cannot by itself establish what was present, or whether anything existed beyond the mind.

Still, these experiences invite us to consider whether our ordinary understanding of perception captures the full range of human consciousness. There may be dimensions of our awareness that we have scarcely begun to understand.
