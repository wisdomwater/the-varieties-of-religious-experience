# Day 37 — Living with an Unquiet Heart

*The deepest weariness of a divided life may come from the inability to find peace within oneself, whatever one's outward circumstances.*

There are forms of unhappiness that arise from misfortune, disappointment, or the loss of something we cherish. Yet another kind of suffering emerges when the individual becomes divided against himself. In this condition, even favorable circumstances may provide little satisfaction, because the difficulty lies not primarily in the world outside but in the conflicting demands of one's own consciousness.

The experience of Henry Alline offers a remarkable illustration. As a young man, he enjoyed the companionship and admiration of his acquaintances, frequently participating in their gatherings and amusements. Yet alongside these ordinary pleasures, he carried an increasingly troubled conscience. His religious aspirations had become sufficiently powerful to make his familiar activities seem unworthy, but not sufficiently commanding to draw him into a different manner of life.

What makes his account particularly moving is the contrast between his public appearance and private experience. He sometimes encouraged the merriment of his companions while inwardly longing to escape their company. He maintained an appearance of cheerfulness because he feared that others might discover his distress. Even when he was outwardly participating in the activities of ordinary life, his inward attention remained occupied by anxiety, remorse, and the effort to preserve his standing among those around him.

We should observe that the activities troubling Alline were not necessarily serious moral offenses. His suffering arose largely from the relationship between his conduct and his conception of what his life ought to become. The same amusements that appeared innocent to his companions acquired a painful significance within his own moral and religious imagination.

This reveals an important feature of the divided personality. Inner unrest depends not simply upon what a person does, but upon the discord between the different meanings and values that govern his experience. One may outwardly conform to the expectations of society while feeling inwardly estranged from the life one is leading.

Such suffering can persist even when the individual makes considerable efforts to resolve it. Alline continued his religious duties, examined his conscience, and repeatedly attempted to quiet his distress, yet these efforts did not immediately produce the peace he desired.

His experience prepares us to understand why the eventual unification of personality can bring such extraordinary relief. For someone who has lived amid continual inward conflict, happiness may come to mean something more fundamental than pleasure or favorable fortune: the discovery of a life in which one's deepest convictions and governing impulses no longer stand in constant opposition.
