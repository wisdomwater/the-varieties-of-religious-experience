# Day 68 — The Limits of Self-Sacrifice

*The willingness to give ourselves for others is among humanity's noblest capacities, yet even sacrifice must be guided by an understanding of the good it seeks to accomplish.*

We have encountered individuals whose religious convictions inspire extraordinary generosity, patience, and self-denial. Their willingness to surrender personal advantages, endure suffering, and respond to hostility with kindness seems to reveal possibilities of character far beyond our ordinary expectations. Yet we must now confront a difficult question: can a person become so devoted to self-sacrifice that the very generosity we admire begins to produce harmful consequences?

The difficulty becomes especially apparent when we examine the religious ideal of non-resistance. The saint may believe that responding to aggression with aggression merely perpetuates the hostility from which human beings need deliverance. He therefore offers forgiveness where others demand punishment, trusting that kindness may awaken something better in the person who has caused him harm.

Such conduct possesses an undeniable moral beauty, but its practical consequences are uncertain. A person who refuses to resist exploitation may unintentionally encourage further wrongdoing. A community that extends unlimited trust without considering the character of those receiving it may expose its most vulnerable members to danger.

The philosopher Herbert Spencer argued that the conduct of an ideally good person would be perfectly suited only to an ideally good environment. In a world where selfishness, violence, and dishonesty remain common, behavior founded upon universal goodwill may prove poorly adapted to actual circumstances. The saint may act according to principles suitable for a better world while living among people who have not yet learned to share them.

Yet this observation does not settle the question. If everyone limits generosity to what present circumstances appear to justify, human relationships may never develop beyond their existing condition. Someone must occasionally act upon the possibility that others are capable of becoming better than their previous conduct would suggest.

We know that kindness can sometimes accomplish what punishment and suspicion cannot. Individuals regarded as hopelessly dishonest or hostile have undergone remarkable transformations when another person extended a degree of trust they had not expected to receive. The saint's apparent imprudence may therefore become an instrument of moral change, revealing possibilities that more cautious people would never discover.

This introduces a distinction between the immediate consequences of an action and its larger influence upon human life. A particular act of generosity may fail, leaving its giver deceived or injured. Yet the example of someone willing to risk personal advantage for another's welfare may awaken generosity in others and gradually alter the moral expectations of a community.

We should not pretend that this possibility removes every difficulty. Sacrifice can be wasted, trust can be abused, and the protection of innocent people may sometimes require firm resistance. The value of self-denial cannot be established by the amount of suffering endured or the purity of the intention alone.

Nevertheless, humanity would be impoverished without those willing to venture beyond the limits of ordinary prudence. Their generosity challenges our assumption that the world must always remain as selfish as experience has taught us to expect.

Perhaps the deepest significance of self-sacrifice lies in this willingness to take a risk for the sake of a better possibility. Wisdom must consider the consequences, but courage sometimes requires us to act before the goodness we hope to awaken has become visible.
