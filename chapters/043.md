# Day 43 — The Meaning of Conversion

*Conversion is not merely the acceptance of a new belief, but the transformation of the place that belief occupies within a human life.*

We have examined the divided personality and the remarkable processes through which its conflicting elements may become unified. We must now turn our attention to one of the most significant expressions of this transformation in religious experience: the phenomenon traditionally called conversion. The word is familiar, yet its psychological meaning is considerably richer than the simple act of joining a religious community or accepting a particular system of doctrines.

To be converted, regenerated, or born anew is to undergo a change through which a previously divided and dissatisfied individual comes to experience a greater unity of purpose and a new sense of inward assurance. Such a transformation may occur gradually, through the slow development of character, or suddenly, in an experience that seems to divide an entire lifetime into a before and an after.

The essential feature is the altered relationship between religious convictions and the rest of the personality. A person may have possessed religious beliefs for many years without allowing them to become the governing influence of his existence. He may have understood their teachings, participated in their observances, and even approved of their moral ideals, while continuing to organize his daily life around entirely different concerns.

Conversion occurs when these previously secondary convictions acquire a new authority. What once occupied the margins of consciousness becomes central, while interests that formerly dominated the personality lose some of their power. Religious ideals cease to be merely propositions that the individual acknowledges and become living influences through which he interprets experience and directs his conduct.

This change may be accompanied by powerful emotions, a sense of divine presence, or an overwhelming experience of relief. Yet we should distinguish the psychological fact of transformation from the theological explanation offered for it. Whether a direct divine intervention is necessary to produce conversion remains a question that our initial description cannot settle.

Nor should we imagine that every convert experiences the same degree of happiness or permanent harmony. Human character remains complex, and the consequences of a profound change must be examined rather than assumed.

Our task, therefore, is to understand how religious convictions acquire this transforming power. What allows an idea that was once merely acknowledged to become a source of energy capable of reorganizing an entire life?

In answering this question, we shall discover that conversion concerns not simply what a person believes, but the deeper mystery of how belief becomes experience, and experience becomes character.
