# Preface

### The Fruits of What We Believe

I first encountered William James and *The Varieties of Religious Experience* while exploring near-death experiences, commonly called NDEs. I had become fascinated by accounts of individuals who approached death and returned describing encounters with light, overwhelming love, profound peace, and a sense of belonging to something greater than themselves. Their stories raised questions about consciousness, the possibility of an afterlife, and whether our ordinary understanding of reality captures everything there is to know.

What interested me almost as much as the experiences themselves was the radically different ways people interpreted them. Some regarded these accounts as evidence that death opens into a reality characterized by unconditional love and reconciliation. Others, often approaching the subject from a more traditional religious perspective, suggested that such experiences could be deceptions originating from Satan, intended to lead people away from the truth.

I found myself wondering how two people could examine similar accounts and arrive at such opposing conclusions. Both believed they were taking spiritual reality seriously, and both could appeal to theological arguments in support of their interpretations. Yet neither possessed the kind of conclusive evidence that could settle the question beyond reasonable doubt.

This led me to a deeper question. When certainty is unavailable, how should we decide what deserves our belief?

I began considering the consequences of the beliefs themselves. If one interpretation encourages greater love, compassion, peace, and openness toward others, while another cultivates fear, suspicion, and distrust, should those differences influence our judgment? Does the fruit of a belief tell us something about its truth, or does it merely reveal whether we find the belief emotionally satisfying?

I recognized that comforting ideas are not necessarily true, just as uncomfortable truths do not become false because we dislike them. Nevertheless, it seemed unreasonable to disregard the ways our convictions shape our character, relationships, and understanding of the world. Beliefs are not merely propositions we hold in our minds. They become lenses through which we interpret reality, and over time they influence the people we become.

It was during these reflections that I first heard of William James.

### A Different Way of Approaching Religion

William James (1842–1910) was an American philosopher, psychologist, and professor at Harvard University. Widely regarded as one of the founders of modern psychology, he was also a major figure in the development of pragmatism, a philosophical approach concerned with the practical consequences of our ideas and beliefs.

James lived during a period when scientific discoveries were transforming traditional understandings of human nature and the universe. Psychology was emerging as a serious field of inquiry, while religious institutions confronted increasingly difficult questions about the authority of their doctrines. Rather than simply choosing between scientific skepticism and religious certainty, James believed that religious experience itself deserved careful investigation.

In 1901 and 1902, he delivered a series of twenty lectures at the University of Edinburgh as part of the prestigious Gifford Lectures on natural religion. These lectures became *The Varieties of Religious Experience: A Study in Human Nature*, published in 1902.

James originally intended to divide his lectures between a psychological examination of religious experience and a philosophical investigation of its ultimate meaning. Yet he became so absorbed in the experiences themselves that the descriptive portion expanded to fill nearly the entire series.

Instead of beginning with religious doctrines and attempting to demonstrate their correctness, James began with the people who experienced religion. He examined accounts of conversion, mystical encounters, spiritual despair, extraordinary joy, saintliness, and the profound transformations that sometimes follow encounters with what individuals perceive as divine.

He was especially interested in the relationship between the origins of religious experiences and their consequences. An experience might arise under unusual psychological circumstances, but this did not automatically determine its value. James believed that we must also consider what the experience produces in the life of the person who undergoes it.

This distinction became particularly important to me. An explanation of how a belief or experience originates does not necessarily tell us everything about what it means, whether it is valuable, or whether it corresponds to something real.

### Judging by the Fruits

One of James's enduring contributions was his insistence that religious experience should be evaluated, in significant part, through its consequences for human life.

Does a religious conviction deepen compassion? Does it inspire courage, generosity, and patience? Does it help individuals confront suffering without surrendering to despair? Does it encourage a fuller and more meaningful participation in existence?

These questions do not eliminate the importance of evidence or philosophical reasoning. Nor do they establish that every comforting religious experience reveals an objective spiritual reality. James was careful to distinguish the practical value of a belief from the separate question of whether its claims about the universe are ultimately true.

He also recognized that religious devotion can produce troubling consequences. Fanaticism, excessive self-denial, and misplaced certainty may emerge from sincere religious convictions. Judging beliefs by their fruits therefore requires more than asking whether they make us feel good. We must consider their influence upon the whole of human life, including their effects upon others.

Yet James's approach offered me a way to take religious experience seriously without feeling compelled to accept every interpretation attached to it.

I could listen to an account of a near-death experience without immediately deciding whether it demonstrated the existence of heaven, revealed the activity of the subconscious, or represented some other phenomenon that we do not yet understand. I could recognize the significance of the experience, examine its consequences, and remain open to possibilities that extended beyond my existing beliefs.

Perhaps most importantly, I could approach the experiences of others with curiosity rather than suspicion.

### A Book That Continues to Matter

More than a century after its publication, *The Varieties of Religious Experience* remains a foundational work in the psychology of religion. Its influence extends into philosophy, psychology, religious studies, and ongoing discussions about consciousness and spirituality.

Its importance lies partly in the seriousness with which James approached experiences that others might have dismissed as irrational, pathological, or unworthy of scholarly attention. He neither accepted every religious claim at face value nor assumed that a psychological explanation rendered the experience meaningless.

James also challenged the idea that religious truth must always be approached through established institutions or intellectual systems. He directed attention toward the living experiences from which many religious traditions originally emerged, recognizing that human beings encounter the sacred through remarkably different temperaments and circumstances.

His approach helped establish a way of studying religion that takes personal experience seriously while preserving the freedom to question its interpretation. That combination of intellectual openness and critical examination remains valuable in a world where religious certainty and religious skepticism can sometimes become equally resistant to unfamiliar perspectives.

### About This Book

James's original work is substantial, rich with historical examples, philosophical arguments, and psychological observations. Its language and structure reflect the academic world of the early twentieth century, and some readers may find its length and style challenging.

This book is intended as an accessible introduction to his central ideas through one hundred short daily readings.

Rather than reproduce his lectures word for word, I have adapted their themes into contemporary language, preserving the broad movement of his arguments while making them easier to encounter in smaller portions. The readings are written in a voice inspired by James's perspective, often addressing the reader directly, but they are modern interpretations rather than quotations or a replacement for his original work.

Each reading begins with an original reflection and explores a particular aspect of religious experience. Together, they move through the nature of belief, the contrasting experiences of happiness and despair, the divided self, conversion, saintliness, mysticism, philosophical reasoning, and the enduring mystery of consciousness.

My intention is not to persuade readers to adopt a particular religious tradition. Nor is it to suggest that all religious claims are equally true or that the most emotionally satisfying belief must necessarily be the most accurate.

Instead, I hope these readings encourage a more generous and thoughtful approach to the questions that have occupied humanity for centuries. What does it mean to encounter something we regard as sacred? How do our beliefs shape our experience of existence? What can we learn from people whose spiritual lives differ profoundly from our own?

And perhaps most importantly, how can we pursue truth while remaining attentive to the kind of people our pursuit is helping us become?

I began this journey with questions about near-death experiences, heaven, and the possibility of divine deception. Those questions have not all been resolved, but James has helped me appreciate that uncertainty need not lead to fear or intellectual paralysis.

We can examine evidence, acknowledge the limits of our understanding, and remain willing to revise our conclusions. We can recognize the genuine complexity of religious experience without surrendering our capacity for discernment. And we can consider the fruits of our beliefs as meaningful evidence, while remembering that they are only one part of a larger search for truth.

My hope is that these hundred readings offer you something of the same freedom I discovered in James's work: the freedom to question without hostility, to believe without demanding certainty, and to encounter the spiritual experiences of others with humility and curiosity.

For whatever the ultimate nature of reality may be, our understanding of it remains unfinished. And perhaps the willingness to continue learning is itself one of the better fruits of our search.
